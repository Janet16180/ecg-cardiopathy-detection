How does the algorithm choose
a place in the ECG?

It compares repeated beats, finds where their shapes differ most, and returns a beat, lead, and time interval. A separate method estimates the QRS boundaries.

“Place” here means coordinates on the ECG trace. A lead is an electrical view of the heart; it does not identify an anatomical injury. These algorithms suggest where to look.

This candidate localization method uses signal processing and an unsupervised comparison within each recording. It applies a defined statistical recipe rather than training another neural network. Neural networks also use mathematics, but learn their weights during training. The existing screening classifier and its attention maps are separate AI components.

1. Find the repeated beats

The algorithm detects the main spikes, called R peaks. They give it anchors for cutting the continuous recording into comparable beats. It uses the voltages to find these anchors; expert labels are only used later to check the answer.

2. Compare one beat with the other beats

The method subtracts each beat’s local voltage baseline and allows a small alignment correction of up to 10 milliseconds. It then takes the median shape of the other beats. The beat being inspected is excluded from its own reference.

Beat under inspectionReference from the other beats

The reference means “typical of the other beats in this recording.” It is not guaranteed to be clinically normal. A change repeated on every beat can disappear into the reference.

3. Turn the difference into a score

At every sample, the method measures how far the blue line is from the reference. It divides by a typical QRS voltage range for that lead, then squares the difference. This gives a non-negative score and allows comparisons across leads.

sample score = ((beat voltage − reference voltage) / lead scale)²

The red curve is the actual score field used by the sliding window. Gray areas belong to a neighboring beat. A window can cross that boundary and include both beats’ sample scores; its center determines which beat receives the window.

Slide the window

The algorithm averages that sample score within a 140-millisecond window, moving the window in 10-millisecond steps. Move the slider to see how the interval and its average score change.

4. Choose a beat, lead, and interval

The method searches all eligible windows across all leads. A beat receives the largest score from the windows assigned to it by their nearest detected R peak. The highest-scoring beat supplies the saved candidate shown below.

Each cell shows the largest window score for one beat in one lead. Click a cell to inspect that combination. Brightness is relative to the maximum in this example; it is not disease probability.

Moving the controls explores existing scores. It does not retrain the model or change the algorithm’s saved choice.

5. A separate question: where does QRS start and end?

The difference window above has a fixed width. It can fall on QRS, on a recovery wave, or on noise. To draw physiological QRS borders, the separate boundary method follows how quickly the filtered voltage changes.

Follow the local slope

The line below is a smoothed measure of voltage change. The algorithm finds activity connected to the nearby maximum, above 12% of that maximum. It closes small gaps and adds a small margin. It combines both channels using the earliest start and latest end.

The expert timing is joint across the two channels. It validates wave boundaries, not the location of diseased tissue or a pathological lead. Voltages use the database header’s nominal scale.

The first result checks beat identity. The second checks QRS timing. They are separate evaluations. Neither confirms that the selected lead and short window are the exact pathological region.

Remaining limitations: normal-labeled beats can be selected, noise can dominate, and persistent abnormalities can resemble the recording’s own reference. T-wave improvement is still uncertain. These candidates have not become a fully validated clinical localization system.

All displayed waveforms are real. The chosen success and failure examples explain the method; they do not create a new accuracy estimate. No synthetic ECGs, new training, or protected final-test records are used.

Sources, exact method details, and reproducibility

Public recordings: INCART 1.0.0 and QT Database 1.0.0. The project’s Experiments 056 and 059 contain the full benchmark reports.

INCART: native 257 Hz waveform shown; scoring replay uses 500 Hz, 10-second cores with up to ±0.5 seconds of context. Reference beats require complete support. Baseline is the median from R−100 to−60 ms; lead scale is median QRS peak-to-peak voltage, with a 0.1 mV floor. No per-beat amplitude fit. Sample ownership is bounded by neighboring-R midpoints; eligible windows can cross those midpoints. Residual arrays and coordinates return to the original recording timeline.

QTDB: native 250 Hz; full-record 0.5–30 Hz filtering and smoothed absolute derivative. The activity search is restricted to R−120 to+160 ms; the local maximum lies within ±60 ms. Threshold is 12%, gaps are closed over about 10 ms, borders expand by 8 ms. Existing P boundaries are preserved by the full hybrid method.
